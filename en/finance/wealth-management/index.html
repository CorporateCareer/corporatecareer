Wealth Management & Private Banking

Discover what wealth management and private banking are, how the advisory relationship with a client works, what the job involves and how students can prepare for internships and client advisor roles.

Introduction

What is wealth management and private banking?

Wealth management and private banking are about managing the financial affairs of wealthy individuals and families: building and monitoring an investment portfolio, planning for retirement and succession, and being tax-aware about decisions along the way. Private banking is usually the term for this service inside a bank, delivered through a dedicated relationship manager. Wealth management is the broader discipline, which also includes independent advisory firms that are not part of a bank. Both differ from retail banking, which serves the mass market with standardised products, and from asset management, which runs pooled funds and mandates for many clients, including large institutions, rather than advising individuals directly.

Within this broader field, mandates typically fall into two categories. In a discretionary mandate, the wealth manager makes day-to-day investment decisions within agreed parameters, without asking the client to approve every trade. In an advisory mandate, the manager proposes ideas and the client makes the final call on each transaction. Many private banks offer both, and a single client can hold different mandates for different parts of their wealth.

What it isManaging the investments, planning and reporting of wealthy individuals and families through a dedicated advisor relationship.
How firms earnMainly through fees on the assets they manage for a client, sometimes supplemented with advisory or transaction fees.
Why clients use itManaging significant wealth well takes time, expertise and coordination across investing, tax and estate planning that most people prefer to delegate.
Why students find it attractiveIt combines financial knowledge with real client relationships, offers a more predictable path than dealmaking roles, and rewards trust built over years.

Step by step

How the advisory relationship works

The cycle below repeats many times over a client relationship, but the first pass through it is the most intensive. Onboarding a new client usually involves gathering a fuller picture than any single later review, including existing holdings, liabilities, other advisors already involved, and family circumstances that could affect the plan later, such as an expected inheritance or a business succession.

1 Get to know the clientThe advisor maps the client's goals, time horizon, family situation and risk appetite.
2 Build a tailored strategyA proposed investment strategy and financial plan is put together for the client to review.
3 Implement the portfolioOnce agreed, the strategy is put into practice across investments and planning steps.
4 Monitor and reportThe portfolio is tracked and the client receives regular, clear reporting on performance.
5 Review and rebalanceRegular review meetings check whether the strategy still fits and adjust it where needed.
6 Adapt over timeAs life events happen, retirement, inheritance, a business sale, the plan evolves with the client.

The relationship

A long-term relationship, not a single transaction

Unlike a single deal or trade, the value in wealth management comes from a relationship that runs for years, sometimes across generations of the same family. An advisor gets to know a client's full situation, not just their portfolio, and adjusts the plan as circumstances change.

That is also why trust and discretion matter so much in this line of work. Clients share sensitive information about their finances and their families, and the relationship only works if that trust is maintained over time.

Regulation shapes the relationship too. Firms are required to understand a client's financial situation, objectives and risk tolerance before giving advice, a process usually described as client suitability or know-your-client screening, and to review that picture periodically rather than only at onboarding. An advisor employed by a bank typically works within that bank's approved investment universe and house view, while an advisor at an independent firm may have more latitude to select products from across the market.

Types

Types of wealth management providers

Private banksThe dedicated private banking arm of a universal bank, serving wealthy clients alongside the bank's retail and institutional business.
Independent wealth managers and multi-family officesFirms outside the banking system that advise multiple wealthy families or individuals, sometimes with more flexibility on products.
Boutique private banksSmaller, specialised institutions focused entirely on private banking, often with a longer history and a more personal service model.

The distinction between these models often comes down to product access as much as brand. A bank-owned private bank may lean toward its own investment funds and structured products, sometimes called a closed or partly open architecture. Independent wealth managers and multi-family offices are more likely to run an open architecture, selecting funds and managers from across the market rather than a single house, though this varies firm by firm and is worth asking about directly.

This is different from a single-family office, which manages the wealth of just one family rather than many clients. Single-family offices are a related but distinct part of the wealth landscape and are not covered in detail on this page.

Career timeline

How long each step typically takes

There is no single fixed ladder in wealth management, and timing depends heavily on the firm, the country and how someone enters the field. As a rough, qualitative guide, the path from a first role to a senior position tends to unfold gradually, since building the trust a client relationship depends on simply takes time.

Junior roles focus on learning the products, tools and internal processes, and typically last a few years before someone starts managing their own client relationships. Moving from a private banker role to a senior or team lead position usually depends more on demonstrated relationship skills and a track record with clients than on a fixed number of years.

The business model

How wealth management firms make money

Most wealth management revenue comes from management fees charged on the assets a firm looks after for a client, calculated as a share of the portfolio's value. This ties the firm's income directly to how much wealth it manages and how well it retains clients over time.

Some firms also charge advisory or transaction fees for specific services or trades, but the core of the model is recurring: income grows by attracting new client assets, growing existing portfolios and keeping clients over the long run, rather than by closing individual deals.

This is a meaningful contrast with deal-driven parts of finance such as investment banking or private equity, where income depends on completing specific transactions. In wealth management, the incentive is aligned with keeping a client's relationship, and their assets, with the firm for as long as possible.

The fee structure often mirrors the mandate type. A discretionary mandate typically carries a single all-in fee calculated on assets under management, covering advice, execution and custody together. An advisory mandate may combine a lower ongoing fee with separate charges each time a transaction is executed, since the client is deciding, and paying for, each trade individually. Custody and administration of the assets is usually billed separately again, whichever mandate is used.

Compensation

How compensation typically works

Compensation in wealth management is typically built around a base salary, with a bonus component layered on top. The size and structure of that bonus varies by firm, but it is often linked to factors such as the assets an advisor manages, the strength and growth of their client relationships, or the performance of the firm as a whole.

The exact mix also depends on the type of firm. Advisors employed by a bank are usually salaried staff whose bonus reflects team and firm-wide outcomes as much as their individual book, while advisors at some independent firms may work on a model closer to partnership, where their own client base has a more direct link to what they earn. Neither structure is inherently better, and the choice often says more about risk appetite and preferred working environment than about the work itself.

Because income for the firm is tied to recurring fees rather than one-off deals, compensation tends to build more gradually over a career than in deal-driven roles, rewarding advisors who retain and grow client relationships over many years.

Exact figures are not something we publish here, since they vary widely by firm, seniority and client segment. Students preparing for this path should focus less on chasing a number and more on understanding how the relationship-driven model rewards trust and consistency over time.

Skills

Skills you need

The technical side, portfolio construction, tax and planning concepts, can be learned on the job and through study. What is harder to teach is the judgement to translate a client's sometimes vague goals into a concrete plan, and the discipline to deliver difficult news, such as a market downturn, in a way that keeps trust intact rather than eroding it.

Compare

Wealth management versus other finance paths

Wealth management vs asset management

Wealth management advises individual clients directly and centres on the relationship. Asset management runs pooled funds and mandates for many clients at once, including large institutions, with far less direct personal contact. A wealth manager typically builds a bespoke portfolio around one client's specific goals and constraints, while an asset manager runs a standard strategy inside a fund structure that many investors buy into on the same terms.

Wealth management vs family office

A wealth manager serves many client relationships at once. A single-family office serves only one family, dedicated entirely to that family's wealth and affairs.

Wealth management vs retail banking

Retail banking serves the mass market with standardised products at scale. Wealth management is high-touch, tailored advice built around a smaller number of wealthy clients. Eligibility for private banking is usually based on a minimum level of investable assets set by each firm, though the precise threshold is not something we publish here since it varies by institution.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is private banking?

Private banking is the part of a bank or wealth manager that looks after the financial affairs of wealthy individuals and families, from investment portfolios to estate and tax-aware planning, delivered through a dedicated relationship manager.

How is wealth management different from asset management?

Wealth management advises individual clients directly on their personal financial situation and relationship. Asset management runs pooled funds and mandates for many clients at once, including large institutions, with less direct personal contact.

Do you need a specific degree?

Most entrants have a background in finance, economics or business, but there is no single required degree. Strong analytical skills and comfort working with clients matter as much as the exact subject studied.

What does a typical day look like?

A mix of client meetings and calls, portfolio and market research, preparing reporting materials, and coordinating with colleagues on planning, tax or legal questions. The balance shifts with seniority.

Is it client-facing or more analytical work?

Both, though the mix changes over a career. Juniors spend more time on research and materials, while senior private bankers spend more time directly with clients building and maintaining the relationship.

What qualifications help, such as a CFA?

A CFA charter or a comparable investment or financial planning qualification is valued and often pursued over the first years of a career, alongside internal training programmes most banks run for new advisors.

What is the work-life balance like?

Generally more predictable than deal-driven roles such as investment banking or private equity, since the work is relationship and advisory in nature rather than built around transaction deadlines, though this varies by firm and role.

Can you move between wealth management and other finance paths?

Yes. People move in from retail banking, asset management or financial planning, and experienced private bankers sometimes move toward asset management or independent advisory roles later in their career.
