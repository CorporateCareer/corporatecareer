Finance career path

Mergers & Acquisitions (M&A)

Learn what M&A advisory is, the difference between buy-side and sell-side, what the roles pay, which exit routes it opens, and how students can prepare.

View vacanciesExplore finance paths

Introduction

What is M&A advisory?

Mergers and acquisitions, usually shortened to M&A, is the part of investment banking that advises companies on buying, selling or merging with other businesses. M&A advisers guide a client through the whole transaction: they value the business, find or approach the other side, run the process, and help negotiate the price and the terms. They earn a fee for the advice and for getting the deal done, rather than investing their own money.

The fee itself usually has two parts: a retainer paid regardless of outcome, and a success fee, often the larger piece, paid only if the transaction closes. That structure aligns the adviser with getting the deal done, but it also means a firm can spend months on a mandate and earn very little if the deal falls apart. On a sell-side mandate the adviser typically also runs a formal or informal market check, contacting a shortlist or a wider group of potential buyers to test appetite and put competitive tension on price, even when a seller already has one interested party in mind.

What it isAdvising companies on buying, selling or merging, and running the transaction from start to close.
How advisers earnMostly through a success fee when the deal closes, plus retainers for the work along the way.
Why companies use itFor a fair valuation, access to the right buyers or targets, and someone to run a complex process.
Why students like itA steep learning curve, real deal experience early, strong exit options and high pay, in exchange for long hours.

Types of deal

The main types of M&A

M&A is a catch-all term for several kinds of transaction, which differ in structure, in how the two companies relate, and in why the deal happens.

MergerTwo companies agree to combine into a single new entity, usually presented as a combination of equals.
AcquisitionOne company buys a controlling stake in another. The target may keep its name and structure or be absorbed.
TakeoverAn acquisition that can be friendly, agreed with the target board, or hostile, taken directly to shareholders over the board objection.
Spin-offA company separates part of itself into a standalone business, by selling it or distributing its shares to existing shareholders.

Deals are also described by how the two businesses relate to each other:

HorizontalBetween competitors in the same industry, usually to gain scale, market share or cost savings.
VerticalAlong the supply chain, when a company buys a supplier or a customer to control more of its value chain.
ConglomerateBetween unrelated businesses, usually to diversify earnings across different markets.

The motives behind a deal usually come down to a few themes: accelerating growth or entering a new market faster than building from scratch, gaining scale and cutting cost, acquiring talent or technology, spreading risk away from a single product or region, or picking up an undervalued asset at an attractive price.

Two mandates

Buy-side and sell-side

Every M&A mandate falls on one of two sides. The side you are on shapes the work, the process and who you answer to.

Sell-sideAdvising a company or its owners on a sale. The adviser prepares the marketing materials, builds a list of likely buyers, runs the auction and works to get the best price and terms for the seller.
Buy-sideAdvising an acquirer that wants to grow through acquisition. The adviser screens and identifies targets, values them, tests how the deal affects earnings, and helps structure and negotiate the offer.

In practice a sell-side process runs as a broad auction, a controlled auction with a shortlist of the most credible buyers, or a bilateral negotiation with a single party, and the choice affects both the price tension and the speed of the deal. A broad auction usually produces the strongest price but takes longer and risks confidentiality leaking into the market; a negotiated sale with one buyer is faster and more discreet but gives the seller less leverage. On the buy-side, the adviser distinguishes between a strategic buyer, a competitor or adjacent company buying for synergies and long-term fit, and a financial buyer such as a private equity fund buying primarily for a financial return, since the two negotiate very differently.

Step by step

How an M&A deal comes together

Most M&A processes move through a similar path, from winning the mandate to signing and closing. Junior bankers do much of the analysis, the modelling and the documents along the way.

Behind the simplified diagram sit a few mechanics that shape timing and risk. Once a buyer is selected, the parties often sign an exclusivity agreement that stops the seller talking to other bidders for a set period while the buyer completes diligence and negotiates the share purchase agreement, the contract that sets out price, warranties and indemnities. Larger or cross-border deals also need merger control clearance from competition authorities before they can close, which can add months to the timeline and is one of the main reasons a signed deal is not the same as a closed deal.

Roles

M&A roles

M&A teams are structured as a pyramid, with several analysts and associates supporting each vice president and a small number of managing directors at the top who own the client relationships. That structure means the day-to-day workload is heaviest at the junior levels, while seniority is measured less by technical skill and more by the ability to originate mandates and manage clients.

Career timeline

How long each step takes

M&A has one of the clearest career ladders in finance. Most people start as an analyst straight from university, usually for two to three years, or join as an associate after an MBA. Progress depends on performance, and the hours are long, especially in the early years.

From vice president upward the focus shifts from doing the analysis to managing clients and winning mandates. Reaching managing director, the top of the group, usually takes well over a decade in the industry. Many people move on before then, which is one of the strengths of the path.

Many banks run an informal up-or-out expectation at the analyst and associate levels: people who are not on track for promotion are often encouraged to move on rather than stay in place for years. This is one reason the exit opportunities below matter so much, since a large share of any analyst class will leave the industry, or move to another bank, well before reaching managing director.

Compensation

M&A compensation

M&A is well known for high pay, made up of a base salary and a bonus that grows quickly with seniority and with the deals that close. The trade-off is the hours, which are long, often seventy to eighty a week for juniors and sometimes far more near a deadline, with late nights and weekends during live deals.

The figures below are a rough guide for large banks in the United States, where pay is highest. In Europe, including the Netherlands, it is typically lower, and part of the bonus is often deferred at senior levels. Treat them as an illustration, not a promise.

LevelTotal compensation
Analyst$180,000 to $220,000
Associate$285,000 to $500,000
Vice President$525,000 to $800,000
Managing Director$800,000 and up

Figures are illustrative, in US dollars, for large US banks, and include base plus bonus. European pay is generally lower.

A day in the life

A typical day for a junior M&A banker

Much of the day goes into models and documents. A junior updates the valuation, works on the teaser and the information memorandum, builds slides for a client meeting, and answers questions coming out of the data room. On a live deal there is constant back and forth with lawyers, accountants and the other side.

The pace can change quickly with client requests and with where a deal stands, and the work often stretches into the evening, especially near signing. Analysts regularly report very long weeks, sometimes close to a hundred hours during a busy deal. It is demanding, but it gives early exposure to real transactions and to how companies are bought and sold.

After M&A

Where an M&A background leads

One reason students target M&A is where it takes them next. A few years of deal work opens some of the most sought-after moves in finance.

Private equity funds recruit heavily from M&A specifically because the modelling overlaps so closely: an M&A analyst who has built accretion and dilution and merger models already understands most of the mechanics of an LBO model, which is the core tool used to underwrite a buyout. The main adjustment moving to PE is less about the modelling itself and more about shifting from advising on other people’s capital to being responsible for how the fund’s own money performs over a multi-year hold.

Private equityThe classic exit. Funds value the modelling and deal experience of former M&A analysts.
Corporate developmentRunning acquisitions in-house for a large company, a common and steadier move. The role usually means fewer deals per year than in advisory, but far more involvement in what happens after signing, including integration, since the team stays with the business it just bought rather than moving on to the next mandate.
Equity researchAnalysing listed companies for investors, close to the valuation work of M&A.
Venture capital and growthSome move to growth or venture funds, especially with a sector focus.
In-house strategy or financeA move into strategy, corporate finance or an operating role at a company.
Staying in M&AMany stay and build a long-term career, up to managing director.

Getting in

Recruiting timing varies more by region and firm type than the list below suggests. Large banks in the US often lock in next year’s analyst class through summer internships more than a year in advance, while European offices and boutique advisers frequently hire on a rolling basis closer to the actual start date, which is one reason off-cycle internships and direct applications matter more outside the US.

Compare

M&A versus other paths

M&A vs the rest of investment banking

M&A advises on buying and selling companies. Other divisions, like ECM and DCM, raise money through shares and bonds. ECM leads IPOs and share issuances, DCM structures loans and bonds, and both deal mainly with institutional investors as the counterparty. M&A instead works with a single buyer or seller at a time, and the mandate ends once the deal closes, rather than continuing as an ongoing market relationship.

M&A vs private equity

M&A advises clients on deals for a fee. Private equity invests its own fund, buys companies and earns by improving and selling them. That difference in risk is fundamental: an M&A adviser earns a fee regardless of how the deal performs afterwards, while a PE fund only sees a return once it sells the portfolio company years later, at a profit or a loss. Many private equity professionals start in M&A first specifically to build deal skills before taking on the risk of their own capital.

M&A vs corporate development

Corporate development runs deals in-house for one company. M&A advises many companies from the outside. A corporate development team works on a small number of deals a year for a single buyer, and usually sits close to that company’s strategy function. An M&A adviser runs mandates for several clients at once, which gives more variety but less ownership of the long-term strategy behind any one deal.

M&A vs transaction services

Transaction services runs financial due diligence on a deal. M&A leads the whole process and the negotiation. TS teams, often at the large accounting firms, deliver a detailed due diligence report on the target’s numbers but do not take a position on price or negotiate on the client’s behalf. The M&A adviser uses that report as ammunition, to adjust the terms or defend the price.

M&A vs consulting

Consulting advises on strategy and operations. M&A focuses on the transaction and getting it done. Consultants are usually paid by the hour or by project regardless of the outcome, while an M&A adviser earns most of the fee only if the deal actually closes. Consulting work can still trigger a deal, for example a strategic review that recommends divesting a division, after which an M&A team takes over running the actual sale process.

M&A vs valuation

A valuation specialist produces an independent opinion on what a business or asset is worth, often for a fairness opinion, a tax purpose or a dispute, without running a sale process. An M&A adviser also values the business, but as one step inside a broader mandate that includes finding the counterparty, negotiating and closing.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is M&A advisory?

M&A advisory is the part of investment banking that advises companies on buying, selling or merging with other businesses. The adviser values the company, finds or approaches the other side, runs the process and helps negotiate the deal, in return for a fee.

What is the difference between buy-side and sell-side?

Sell-side means advising a company that is being sold: preparing the materials, finding buyers and running the auction. Buy-side means advising an acquirer: identifying targets, valuing them and helping structure and negotiate the offer.

What does an M&A analyst do?

Analysts build valuation and M&A models, run accretion and dilution analysis, prepare teasers and information memoranda, support due diligence and put together client presentations. It is analytical, detail-heavy work with long hours.

How many hours do M&A bankers work?

Junior bankers often work seventy to eighty hours a week, and analysts sometimes report close to a hundred hours during a busy deal, with late nights and weekends. Hours usually improve at more senior levels.

What are the exit opportunities from M&A?

Common moves are private equity, corporate development, equity research, venture and growth funds, and in-house strategy or finance roles. Many people also stay and build a long-term career in M&A.

What skills do I need for M&A?

Strong financial modelling, valuation and M&A modelling, a solid grasp of accounting and financial analysis, attention to detail, clear communication, negotiation sense and the ability to work under pressure.

Do you need an MBA for M&A?

Not to start as an analyst, which is usually filled straight from university. An MBA is the classic route into the associate level and can speed up progression.

How do I get an M&A internship?

Apply early through spring weeks and summer programmes, recruit at target universities, consider boutique advisers as a less crowded way in, network with bankers and alumni, and prepare hard for technical interviews.
