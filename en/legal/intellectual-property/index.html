Legal career path

Intellectual Property

Discover what intellectual property means in legal practice: how lawyers protect and enforce patents, trademarks and copyright, what they do day to day, the career ladder, and how students prepare for an internship or traineeship.

View vacanciesExplore legal paths

Introduction

What is intellectual property?

Intellectual property, often abbreviated to IP, is the practice area centred on protecting creations of the mind: inventions, brands, works and trade secrets. It gives a creator or holder the exclusive right to use, sell or license something.

IP consists of several sub-rights, each with its own registration and protection system: patents for inventions, trademarks for identifying signs, copyright for works, and design rights for the appearance of products. Trade secrets are protected separately, without registration.

IP lawyers often work with other practice groups: an IP issue in an acquisition touches corporate/M&A, a licence agreement touches commercial, and an IP dispute before the court touches dispute resolution.

What it isThe practice area covering the protection of inventions, trademarks, works and trade secrets.
What it centres onRegistering, monitoring, licensing and enforcing intellectual property rights.
Why clients need itTo protect innovations, brands and creative works against unauthorised use.
Why students find it attractiveThe combination of technology, creativity and legal precision in one field.

At international firms you often work on large, cross-border IP portfolios and disputes. Boutique firms specialise fully in IP and often build deep technical knowledge alongside legal expertise. At Dutch full-service firms, the IP practice often sits close to the corporate or commercial practice.

What they do

What IP lawyers do

The work differs by matter, but the building blocks below come up in almost every IP issue.

Filing and defending patentsRegistering inventions and defending patent rights against infringement.
Registering and monitoring trademarksBuilding trademark portfolios and monitoring them against unauthorised use by third parties.
Handling copyright disputesAssessing disputes over the use of works without the creator's permission.
Protecting trade secretsProtecting confidential business information against misuse or disclosure.
Drafting licence agreementsSetting out the terms under which a party may use another's IP rights.
Conducting infringement proceedingsLitigating on behalf of the rights holder or the alleged infringer over an IP right.
IP portfolio due diligenceScreening a company's IP rights during an acquisition or investment.
Coordinating with other practice groupsWorking with corporate, commercial and dispute resolution when a matter overlaps.

Legal framework

The key legislation

Intellectual property revolves around a set of core statutes that keep recurring, even though the applicable law differs greatly by type of IP right.

Dutch Patents Act 1995Governs the grant and protection of patents in the Netherlands.
Benelux Convention on Intellectual Property (BCIP)Governs trademark and design law jointly for the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg.
Dutch Copyright ActGoverns copyright in works of literature, science and art.
Trade Secrets Protection ActGoverns the protection of confidential business information against unlawful use.
Agreement on a Unified Patent Court (UPC)Governs the European unified patent court for European patents with unitary effect.
EU Trademark RegulationGoverns the registration and protection of the EU trademark across the European Union.

Step by step

How a patent application unfolds

Most patent applications follow a similar path, from filing to grant. For more complex inventions, the prior art search can take considerable time.

Who does which step depends on seniority. Junior lawyers map the prior art and work out the application in detail, while senior lawyers set strategy and conduct opposition proceedings.

Not every application proceeds unopposed: third parties can file an opposition after publication, which can significantly extend the process before the patent is finally granted.

Career timeline

How long each step takes

The legal profession has a fixed statutory basis for the first step: a traineeship in the Netherlands generally lasts three years and is mandatory for every starting lawyer, regardless of practice area. After that, the pace of progression depends heavily on the firm and on individual performance.

After the traineeship, the emphasis shifts from building files to independently leading proceedings and setting IP strategy. Making partner takes more than ten years of legal experience at most firms.

Not everyone climbs the whole ladder at the same firm. Some lawyers move into an in-house IP role at a technology company, bringing their experience with them.

LevelTypical time in role

Advocaat / Associate3 to 5 years

Compensation

Compensation in the legal profession

Compensation in the legal profession usually consists of a fixed salary that rises with seniority, and at some firms a variable bonus tied to individual and firm performance. Intellectual property does not differ materially in pay structure from other practice areas at the same firm.

The ranges below are general indications for larger Dutch and international firms, and do not apply specifically to this practice area or to any particular firm. Actual pay varies by firm, region and role, and is listed where known in the individual vacancies on this site.

From partner level, the nature of compensation changes. A partner typically shares in the firm's profits rather than receiving a fixed salary, which ties pay more closely to their own client portfolio and the results of the practice group as a whole.

A day in the life

A typical day for a junior lawyer

The day often starts with reviewing a patent application or preparing advice on a trademark infringement. On days without a client meeting, much of the time goes into drafting applications and searching the prior art.

An IP lawyer often works on several matters at once, from a patent application to an ongoing infringement proceeding. That requires strict planning, since IP deadlines are often set internationally and a missed deadline can cause a right to lapse.

On a day with a client meeting or hearing, the pace shifts entirely. The lawyer prepares the position, anticipates the other side, and explains technical subject matter in plain terms.

Juniors

What trainee lawyers do

Junior lawyers carry much of the research and drafting work an IP opinion or proceeding rests on, under the supervision of a supervising partner.

  • Researching the prior art
  • Drafting draft applications
  • Case law and literature research
  • Searching trademark registers
  • Drafting draft advice
  • Tracking registration deadlines
  • Sitting in on client meetings
  • Correspondence with patent offices

Much of this work goes back and forth several times between trainee and supervisor before an application or submission is filed. That repetition is not inefficiency, it is how a junior lawyer learns which arguments in an IP matter really matter.

Compare

Intellectual Property versus other practice areas

These practice areas often overlap in the same matters, which is why students sometimes mix them up. The distinction mainly lies in which right is at the centre and who the counterparty is.

Intellectual Property vs Commercial

Commercial drafts the contract. Intellectual Property assesses which IP rights are being licensed or transferred within it.

Intellectual Property vs Corporate/M&A

Corporate/M&A guides the acquisition itself. Intellectual Property assesses the IP portfolio as part of due diligence.

Intellectual Property vs Dispute Resolution

Dispute Resolution conducts the proceeding once an IP dispute escalates to court. Intellectual Property handles the substantive IP strategy around it.

Intellectual Property vs Antitrust, Competition & Trade

Antitrust assesses whether a licence restricts competition. Intellectual Property assesses the validity and scope of the underlying right.

Intellectual Property vs Tax

Tax structures how royalty income from IP rights is treated for tax purposes. Intellectual Property protects and enforces the underlying right itself.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is intellectual property?

Intellectual property is the practice area centred on protecting inventions, trademarks, works and trade secrets against unauthorised use.

What is the difference between a patent and a trademark?

A patent protects a technical invention for a limited period. A trademark protects an identifying sign, such as a name or logo, and can in principle be renewed indefinitely as long as it is used.

What does an IP lawyer do day to day?

IP lawyers file patents and trademarks, monitor portfolios, draft licence agreements, conduct infringement proceedings, and carry out due diligence during acquisitions.

What is the Unified Patent Court (UPC)?

The Unified Patent Court is a joint judicial body for participating EU member states that rules on European patents with unitary effect, instead of separate proceedings per country.

How are trade secrets protected without registration?

Trade secrets are protected as long as the information remains secret, has economic value, and the holder takes reasonable measures to keep it confidential, without any registration being required.

Which firms have an IP practice in the Netherlands?

A selection of international and boutique firms with a verified IP practice is listed on this page. We only name firms whose practice group has been confirmed.

What skills do you need for intellectual property?

Analytical thinking, precise writing, technical affinity, attention to detail, and the ability to work under the pressure of international deadlines.

How do I get into this field as a student?

Take electives in intellectual property, do an internship at a firm or patent office, and apply to structured traineeships that offer a rotation in this field.
