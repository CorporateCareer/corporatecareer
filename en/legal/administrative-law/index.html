Legal career path

Administrative Law

Discover what administrative law, known in the Netherlands as bestuursrecht, involves: the practice areas, what lawyers do day to day, the career ladder, and how students prepare for an internship or traineeship.

View vacanciesExplore legal paths

Introduction

What is administrative law (bestuursrecht)?

Administrative law, known in the Netherlands as bestuursrecht, governs the relationship between citizens and companies on one side and government bodies on the other. It centres on decisions by municipalities, provinces, ministries and independent administrative bodies: permits, subsidies, benefits, enforcement measures and other government decisions that directly affect what companies and citizens may and may not do.

The foundation of administrative law lies in the General Administrative Law Act, the Awb. This act sets out how a decision must come about, which general principles of proper administration a government body must observe, and how someone who disagrees with a decision can challenge it through objection and appeal. Almost every government decision that affects a company or citizen falls under the Awb's rules at some point.

Administrative law is not an isolated field, it runs through other practice areas. An environmental permit for a new data centre, the subsidy scheme for an energy project, or a sanction from a regulator against a financial institution, they all largely play out within administrative law, even though the underlying matter looks at first glance like a real estate, energy or financial file.

What it isThe field of law that governs government decisions and the relationship between government and citizens or companies.
What it centres onPermits, subsidies, enforcement, and the procedures for challenging a decision.
Why clients need itTo obtain a permit, challenge a sanction, or structure a government decision correctly and on time.
Why students find it attractiveDirect societal relevance, intellectually sharp cases, and early ownership of files.

The practice

Three places to practise administrative law

The breakdown below mainly says something about the client you serve and which side of the table you sit on, not about the quality of the work. The same matter, for example a contested environmental permit, can come up at a specialist public law practice, a full-service firm or on the government side, each with a slightly different angle.

Landsadvocaat and specialist public law practiceFirms that predominantly or exclusively advise and represent governments and public institutions, such as Pels Rijcken.
Full-service firm with an administrative law practiceAn administrative law section within a broader firm, mainly supporting companies against the government.
In-house at a government body or companyLawyers employed by a municipality, ministry, regulator or company that itself deals heavily with permits and regulation.

At Pels Rijcken, which has held the office of Landsadvocaat since 1969, the emphasis is on representing the State and other government bodies. At full-service firms such as AKD, administrative law often sits alongside real estate and environmental law, because permits and construction projects are closely linked. In-house, you work closer to the day-to-day decision-making, and see how a permit or subsidy is prepared from the inside, rather than being brought in only after a decision has already been made.

What they do

What administrative lawyers do

The work differs by file, but the building blocks below recur in almost every administrative law case. A lawyer usually works on several files at once, from a pending permit application to a case already before the court.

Legal researchResearching the legislation, policy rules and case law that apply to a file.
Drafting legal opinionsAdvising clients in writing on the feasibility and risks of a permit or decision.
Drafting objection noticesFormally objecting to a decision with the government body that took it.
Drafting appeal briefsBringing a case before the district court or the Council of State once the objection stage is complete.
Supporting permit applicationsStructuring an application so it meets the statutory requirements and holds up under objection.
Litigating before administrative courtsRepresenting clients at a hearing before the district court or a higher administrative court.
Advising government bodiesHelping municipalities and other government bodies draft a decision that is legally sound.
Coordinating with other expertiseWorking with planning lawyers, ecologists or tax specialists when a file touches multiple fields.

Legal framework

The key legislation

Administrative law revolves around a limited set of core statutes that keep recurring, even though the substance differs sharply from file to file. Knowing these statutes quickly explains why a case is approached in a particular way.

General Administrative Law Act (Awb)The general procedural rules for decisions, objections and appeals, and the principles of proper administration.
Environment and Planning ActSince 2024, the consolidated framework for environmental permits, construction, the environment and spatial planning.
Bibob ActGives government bodies the power to refuse or withdraw a permit where there is an integrity risk.
Open Government Act (Woo)Governs the disclosure of government information and requests from citizens and companies for it.
Public Procurement Act 2012Relevant to administrative law where public contracts and permitting intersect.
EU state aid rulesDetermine when government support to a company is permitted, relevant in subsidy disputes.

Practice areas

The main sub-areas within administrative law

Administrative law is broad, and most lawyers specialise within one of the sub-areas below, often combined with an adjacent practice area such as real estate, energy or healthcare.

Environmental and planning lawPermits for construction, environment and spatial planning under the Environment and Planning Act.
Licensing and regulatorySector-specific licences, for example in energy, telecoms or healthcare.
Enforcement and sanctionsOrders subject to penalty, administrative fines and integrity screening under the Bibob Act.
Subsidies and state aidSubsidy disputes and the EU state aid rules that apply to them.
Administrative litigation and judicial reviewObjection and appeal procedures, and interim injunctions in urgent cases.

Step by step

How an administrative law case unfolds

Most cases follow a similar path, from the original decision to a judgment from the highest administrative court. Not every case runs through all the steps; some decisions skip the objection stage and go straight to appeal.

Who does which step depends on seniority and the stage of the case. Junior lawyers write much of the objection and appeal notice and prepare the file, while senior lawyers argue the hearing and set the strategy for the case as a whole.

Some cases end after the objection stage, when the government body amends or withdraws the decision. Cases that do proceed to court can take years if they go on higher appeal to the Administrative Jurisdiction Division of the Council of State, the Central Appeals Tribunal or the Trade and Industry Appeals Tribunal.

Career timeline

How long each step takes

The legal profession has a fixed statutory basis for the first step: in the Netherlands, the traineeship in principle lasts three years and is mandatory for every starting lawyer, regardless of practice area. After that, the pace of progression depends heavily on the firm and on individual performance.

After the traineeship years, the emphasis shifts from learning the craft to independently carrying files. Becoming partner takes upwards of ten years of experience in the profession at most firms, and often comes with a formal nomination by the sitting partners.

Not everyone climbs the whole ladder at the same firm. Some lawyers move after a few years to a government body or a company, for example as an in-house lawyer at a municipality, ministry or a company that deals heavily with permits, bringing the experience of legal practice with them.

Compensation

Compensation in the legal profession

Compensation in the legal profession usually consists of a fixed salary that rises with seniority, and at some firms a variable bonus tied to individual and firm performance. Administrative law does not differ materially in pay structure from other practice areas at the same firm.

The ranges below are general indications for larger Dutch and international firms, and do not apply specifically to administrative law or to any of the firms named on this page. Actual pay varies by firm, region and role, and is listed where known in the individual vacancies on this site.

From partner level, the nature of compensation changes. A partner typically shares in the firm's profits rather than receiving a fixed salary, which ties pay more closely to their own client portfolio and the results of the practice group as a whole.

A day in the life

A typical day for a junior administrative lawyer

The day often starts with going through incoming post and email from government bodies, opposing parties and the court, followed by work on active files. On days without a hearing, much of the time goes to drafting objection or appeal notices, researching case law, and aligning with the client on strategy.

Deadlines play a large role in administrative law: most objection and appeal periods are set by law at six weeks, and missing one can mean a client permanently loses the right to challenge a decision. That pressure makes the work methodical and precise, with little room to slip.

On a hearing day the pace shifts. The lawyer prepares for the oral hearing, aligns with the client on who answers which question if the judge asks, and then argues the case before the district court or, on higher appeal, before the Administrative Jurisdiction Division of the Council of State.

Juniors

What trainee lawyers do

Junior lawyers carry much of the drafting a file rests on, under the supervision of a supervising partner.

  • Drafting objection notices
  • Drafting appeal briefs
  • Case law research
  • Preparing files for a hearing
  • Drafting opinion memos
  • Tracking deadlines and file status
  • Attending hearings
  • Correspondence with government bodies

Much of this work goes back and forth several times between trainee and supervisor before a document goes out. That repetition is not inefficiency, it is how a junior lawyer learns which arguments actually persuade in an administrative law procedure.

Getting in

How to get into administrative law

Most routes run through a law master's with a specialisation in constitutional and administrative law, followed by an internship or traineeship at a firm or government body. A broader law master's with the right electives also works, especially combined with relevant internship experience.

Master's in constitutional and administrative lawThe most direct route, with courses that connect straight to practice.
Internship during the master'sGaining practical experience at a firm or government body before graduating.
Traineeship after graduatingStructured programmes in which you rotate through several practice groups.

Publishing in journalsA case note or article in a journal such as the NJB or AB stands out in applications.

Compare

Administrative law versus other practice areas

These practice areas often overlap in the same files, which is why students sometimes mix them up. The distinction mainly lies in who the counterparty is, which body of law applies, and whether the dispute is against the government or between private parties.

Administrative law vs Dispute Resolution

Administrative law is always against a government body and follows Awb procedures. Dispute Resolution handles disputes between private parties under civil procedure rules.

Administrative law vs Corporate/M&A

Corporate/M&A structures transactions between companies. Administrative law comes into play as soon as a transaction depends on a government permit or approval.

Administrative law vs Public Procurement

Public Procurement focuses specifically on the tender procedure. Administrative law is the broader framework within which procurement decisions can also be challenged.

Administrative law vs ESG

ESG advice concerns companies' sustainability obligations. The environmental and planning permits that follow from them often run through administrative law.

Administrative law vs Tax

Tax law has its own procedural rules with the Tax Authority as the counterparty, but leans heavily on the same Awb principles as broader administrative law.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is administrative law?

Administrative law, known in the Netherlands as bestuursrecht, governs the relationship between citizens and companies on one side and government bodies on the other. It covers decisions such as permits, subsidies, benefits and enforcement measures, and the procedures to challenge them.

What is the difference between an objection and an appeal?

An objection is filed with the government body that took the decision, which then reconsiders it. An appeal is filed with an independent administrative court after the objection has been decided.

What does an administrative lawyer do day to day?

Administrative lawyers draft legal opinions, objection notices and appeal briefs, support permit applications, represent clients at hearings before administrative courts, and advise government bodies on how to draft lawful decisions.

Do you need a specific master's degree for administrative law?

A law degree with a specialisation in constitutional and administrative law is the most direct route, though many administrative lawyers also come from a general Dutch law LLM with the right electives.

What is the difference between administrative law and civil litigation?

Administrative law disputes are always against a government body and follow the procedures of the General Administrative Law Act. Civil litigation resolves disputes between private parties under civil procedure rules.

Which courts handle administrative law cases in the Netherlands?

Cases start at one of the district courts. Higher appeal usually goes to the Administrative Jurisdiction Division of the Council of State, while specialised matters go to the Central Appeals Tribunal or the Trade and Industry Appeals Tribunal.

What skills do you need for administrative law?

Precise legal writing, careful statutory interpretation, analytical thinking, the ability to explain complex rules clearly to clients and officials, and comfort with fixed procedures and deadlines.

How do I get a traineeship in administrative law?

Take electives in constitutional and administrative law, do an internship at a firm or government body with a public law practice, and apply to structured traineeships that offer an administrative law rotation.
