Artificial Intelligence & Digital Regulation

Discover what artificial intelligence & digital regulation means in legal practice: how lawyers advise companies on AI, privacy, data and online platforms, what they do day to day, the career ladder, and how students prepare for an internship or traineeship.

Introduction

What is artificial intelligence & digital regulation?

Artificial intelligence & digital regulation is the fast-growing practice area around the regulation of technology: artificial intelligence, personal data, data and online services. It is strongly EU-driven, with a wave of new legislation that has taken effect in recent years.

The AI Act is the flagship of this field, alongside the General Data Protection Regulation, the Digital Services Act, the Digital Markets Act, the Data Act and the NIS2 Directive on cybersecurity. Together these rules determine how companies may develop, procure and deploy technology.

This field does not stand alone, it touches intellectual property, commercial and competition law. An AI product, a data platform or an online service often raises questions that touch these practice areas at the same time.

What it isThe practice area around the regulation of AI, privacy, data and online platforms.
What it centres onHelping companies develop and deploy technology within the rules.
Why clients need itTo comply with new EU rules, avoid fines and keep users' trust.
Why students find it attractiveA young, fast-changing field at the intersection of law and technology.

The practice

Three settings where you practise this field

The breakdown below mainly says something about the type of employer and the client you serve, not about the quality of the work.

Technology or IP/IT practice at a law firmA technology practice within a broader firm, often close to intellectual property and commercial.
Specialist privacy and technology firmBoutique firms that focus entirely on privacy, data and digital regulation.
In-house at a tech company or regulatorLawyers at a technology company, a platform or a regulator such as the Dutch Data Protection Authority.

At the technology practice of a law firm you often work close to intellectual property and commercial. At specialist privacy and technology firms you focus fully on data and digital regulation. In-house or at a regulator you sit closer to day-to-day decision-making and enforcement. In addition, advisory firms and the Big Four are building large privacy and AI advisory teams.

What they do

What digital regulation lawyers do

The work differs by matter, but the building blocks below come up in almost every technology matter.

Advising on the AI ActAdvising companies on their obligations when developing and deploying AI systems.
Privacy compliance and the GDPRAssessing whether the processing of personal data complies with the General Data Protection Regulation.
Data protection impact assessmentsMapping and mitigating the privacy risks of a new processing activity or technology.
Platform regulation under the DSA and DMAAdvising online platforms on their obligations under the Digital Services Act and Digital Markets Act.
Guiding data breaches and incidentsAssisting a company with a data breach, from notification to communication with the regulator.
Drafting data and AI contractsDrafting data processing agreements and terms for the procurement and supply of AI and data.
Dealing with regulatorsAssisting clients with investigations and enforcement by the Data Protection Authority and other regulators.
Coordinating with other practice groupsWorking with intellectual property, commercial and cybersecurity when a matter has several sides.

Legal framework

The key legislation

This field revolves around a series of mostly European regulations and directives that follow each other quickly and together shape the digital playing field.

AI ActThe European framework for artificial intelligence, with obligations that rise with a system's risk.
General Data Protection RegulationThe GDPR, the core framework for processing personal data in the European Union.
Digital Services ActGoverns the responsibilities of online platforms for content and transparency.
Digital Markets ActImposes obligations on large platforms that act as gatekeepers.
Data Act and Data Governance ActGovern access to and sharing of data between companies and with government.
NIS2 DirectiveSets cybersecurity requirements for companies in essential and important sectors.

Practice areas

The main sub-areas

The field is broad, and most lawyers specialise within one of the sub-areas below.

Privacy and data protectionThe processing of personal data under the GDPR, the largest sub-area.
AI regulationThe obligations around developing and deploying AI under the AI Act.
Platform and market regulationThe obligations of online platforms under the DSA and DMA.
Cybersecurity and data breachesSecurity obligations and incident response under the NIS2 Directive and the GDPR.
Data law and data strategyAccess to, sharing of and ownership of data.

Step by step

How an AI system reaches the market under the AI Act

A high-risk AI system follows a fixed path under the AI Act before it may reach the market and for as long as it is in use. The weight of the obligations depends on the risk a system carries.

Who does which step depends on seniority and the stage. Junior lawyers work out the documentation and the risk analysis, while senior lawyers set the approach and support the client in dealings with the regulator.

Not every system follows the same path: low-risk systems mainly carry transparency obligations, while prohibited uses are not allowed at all. Supervision and enforcement lie with the national regulators and the European AI oversight structure.

Roles

Roles in the technology practice

Each step up the ladder shifts the emphasis further from working out the file toward setting strategy and managing the client relationship. Most lawyers climb this ladder at the same firm, though moving to a technology company or regulator is common at any level.

Supports lawyers during an internship alongside or after studies.

Core skills
Legal research, precision, interest in technology.

The mandatory three-year traineeship straight after graduating.

Career timeline

How long each step takes

The legal profession has a fixed statutory basis for the first step: a traineeship in the Netherlands generally lasts three years and is mandatory for every starting lawyer, regardless of practice area. After that, the pace of progression depends heavily on the firm and on individual performance.

After the traineeship, the emphasis shifts from learning the craft to advising independently on technology and regulation. Making partner takes more than ten years of legal experience at most firms.

Because this is a young field, lawyers regularly move to a technology company, a platform or a regulator, bringing their experience with them. The reverse move happens too.

Compensation

Compensation in the legal profession

Compensation in the legal profession usually consists of a fixed salary that rises with seniority, and at some firms a variable bonus tied to individual and firm performance. This field does not differ materially in pay structure from other practice areas at the same firm.

The ranges below are general indications for larger Dutch and international firms, and do not apply specifically to this practice area or to any particular firm. Actual pay varies by firm, region and role, and is listed where known in the individual vacancies on this site.

From partner level, the nature of compensation changes. A partner typically shares in the firm's profits rather than receiving a fixed salary, which ties pay more closely to their own client portfolio and the results of the practice group as a whole.

A day in the life

A typical day for a junior technology lawyer

The day often starts with reviewing a data processing agreement or working out advice on the obligations of a new AI product. On days without a deadline, much of the time goes into researching new legislation and regulator guidance.

A technology lawyer often works closely with the client's product and engineering teams: the legal requirements have to match how a system actually works. That requires moving between law and technology.

On a day with a data breach, the pace shifts entirely. The lawyer helps determine the scope of the incident, advises on the notification duty, and supports the client in dealings with the Data Protection Authority.

Juniors

What trainee lawyers do

Junior lawyers carry much of the research and drafting a technology opinion rests on, under the supervision of a supervising partner.

  • Researching new legislation and guidance
  • Drafting data processing agreements
  • Mapping data flows
  • Helping work out DPIAs
  • Drafting privacy notices
  • Tracking guidance from the DPA and EDPB
  • Supporting incident response
  • Aligning with product and engineering teams

Much of this work goes back and forth several times between trainee and supervisor before advice goes out. That repetition is not inefficiency, it is how a junior lawyer learns which risks in a technology matter really matter.

Getting in

How you get into this field

Most routes run through a law master's with a specialisation in IT law, intellectual property or privacy law, followed by an internship or traineeship at a firm. An affinity with technology is a plus.

Master's in IT law or intellectual propertyThe most direct route, with courses that tie directly into practice.
Internship during the master'sGaining practical experience at a firm before graduating.
Traineeship after graduatingStructured programmes in which you rotate through several practice groups.
Courses on privacy and technology lawAdvanced electives show affinity with the field.
Earn a privacy certificationA certification such as CIPP/E shows serious interest in privacy law.
Publishing in journalsA case note or article on a technology topic stands out in applications.

Compare

Artificial Intelligence & Digital Regulation versus other practice areas

These practice areas often overlap in the same matters, which is why students sometimes mix them up. The distinction mainly lies in which part of the matter is at the centre.

AI & Digital Regulation vs Intellectual Property

Intellectual Property protects the technology or content itself. AI & Digital Regulation governs how that technology may be deployed and processed.

AI & Digital Regulation vs Commercial

Commercial drafts the commercial contracts. AI & Digital Regulation covers the specific rules on data and AI that appear within them.

AI & Digital Regulation vs Antitrust, Competition & Trade

Antitrust handles market power in general. The Digital Markets Act is a specific digital layer on top of it that this field handles.

AI & Digital Regulation vs Administrative Law

Administrative Law conducts general administrative law. AI & Digital Regulation applies it to work with digital regulators.

AI & Digital Regulation vs ESG

Both are new, EU-driven compliance waves. ESG is about sustainability, this field is about data and technology.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is artificial intelligence & digital regulation?

It is the practice area around the regulation of technology: artificial intelligence, personal data, data and online platforms, largely based on new European legislation.

What is the AI Act?

The AI Act is the European framework for artificial intelligence. It imposes obligations that rise with the risk of an AI system, and prohibits a number of uses entirely.

What is the difference between the GDPR and the AI Act?

The GDPR governs the processing of personal data. The AI Act governs the development and deployment of AI systems, whether or not personal data is involved. In practice they often overlap.

What does a technology lawyer do day to day?

These lawyers advise on the GDPR and the AI Act, work out DPIAs, draft data processing agreements, guide data breaches, and support clients in dealings with regulators.

Do you need a specific master's degree for this field?

A law master's with a specialisation in IT law, intellectual property or privacy law is the most direct route, though many technology lawyers also come from a broad law master's with the right electives.

Which regulators play a role in this field?

The Data Protection Authority supervises the GDPR. In addition, there are regulators for the DSA, the DMA and, with the AI Act, an emerging structure for the oversight of AI.

What skills do you need for this field?

Analytical thinking, precise writing, an affinity with technology, attention to detail, and the ability to connect technology and law clearly.

How do I get into this field as a student?

Take electives in IT and privacy law, do an internship at a firm with a technology practice, and apply to structured traineeships that offer a rotation in this field.
